# Narrative Design Guidelines

## 1. Purpose

This document establishes reusable guidelines for designing, structuring, integrating, and evaluating narrative experiences in video games.

Its purpose is to ensure that stories, characters, dialogue, worldbuilding, and narrative events contribute meaningfully to the game's identity and intended player experience.

These guidelines promote coherent storytelling while preserving player agency, gameplay integration, and creative flexibility.

## 2. Scope

These guidelines apply to:

- Main story and narrative structure.
- Secondary stories and optional narrative content.
- Character design and development.
- Character relationships.
- Dialogue and conversations.
- Worldbuilding and fictional settings.
- Lore and historical background.
- Narrative progression.
- Environmental storytelling.
- Player-driven narrative experiences.
- Narrative choices and consequences.
- Cutscenes and scripted sequences.
- Narrative integration with gameplay.
- Narrative documentation and validation.

These guidelines are genre-independent and MAY be adapted to linear, branching, emergent, environmental, or systemic narrative experiences.

Related specialized topics are covered by:

- Overall game design principles: `design_principles.md`.
- Gameplay systems and player interactions: `gameplay_design_guidelines.md`.
- Core gameplay loops: `core_loop_design_guidelines.md`.
- Spatial and environmental storytelling: `level_design_guidelines.md`.
- Mechanic implementation at the design level: `mechanic_design_guidelines.md`.

## 3. Narrative Design Principles

### 3.1 Narrative Supports the Player Experience

Narrative SHOULD reinforce the intended player experience and the identity of the game.

Designers SHOULD consider:

- What experience the story should create.
- What themes the game explores.
- What emotions the narrative should evoke.
- How the story relates to gameplay.
- What the player should understand, discover, or question.
- How narrative elements contribute to the game world.

Narrative content SHOULD have a clear purpose.

Not every game requires a complex story, extensive lore, or frequent dialogue. Narrative complexity SHOULD be proportional to the experience the game intends to deliver.

### 3.2 Gameplay and Narrative Coherence

Gameplay and narrative SHOULD reinforce each other whenever practical.

Designers SHOULD avoid unnecessary contradictions between:

- What the narrative establishes.
- What the player can actually do.
- How characters behave.
- How the world responds.
- What gameplay systems communicate.
- What the player is expected to believe.

When narrative and gameplay intentionally diverge, the difference SHOULD serve a clear creative purpose.

### 3.3 Player Agency

Narrative design SHOULD respect the player's role in shaping their experience.

Depending on the game, agency MAY involve:

- Choosing dialogue responses.
- Making consequential decisions.
- Exploring narrative information.
- Developing relationships.
- Influencing world events.
- Determining the order of discoveries.
- Interpreting ambiguous situations.

Player agency does not require branching storylines. A linear narrative can still provide meaningful agency through exploration, interpretation, and player participation.

### 3.4 Narrative Consistency

Narrative content MUST remain consistent with established facts unless a deliberate narrative development justifies a change.

Consistency SHOULD be maintained across:

- Character motivations.
- Character histories.
- Relationships.
- World rules.
- Historical events.
- Cultural and social structures.
- Terminology.
- Timeline and chronology.
- Narrative consequences.

Established narrative facts SHOULD be documented to prevent contradictions during production.

### 3.5 Show, Don't Tell

Designers SHOULD communicate narrative information through appropriate combinations of:

- Player actions.
- Environmental details.
- Character behavior.
- Dialogue.
- Visual composition.
- Audio.
- Gameplay consequences.
- Discoverable objects.
- World changes.

Exposition SHOULD be used when direct explanation improves clarity or emotional impact.

Narrative information SHOULD NOT be repeated unnecessarily across multiple channels.

## 4. Narrative Vision and Themes

### 4.1 Narrative Vision

Games with significant narrative content SHOULD establish a clear narrative vision.

The narrative vision SHOULD define:

- The central narrative premise.
- The intended tone.
- The major themes.
- The setting and context.
- The central conflict.
- The intended emotional experience.
- The relationship between narrative and gameplay.

The narrative vision SHOULD support the overall game vision rather than compete with it.

### 4.2 Themes

Themes represent the underlying ideas explored through the narrative.

Examples include:

- Survival.
- Identity.
- Sacrifice.
- Ambition.
- Power.
- Loss.
- Belonging.
- Freedom.
- Responsibility.
- Social conflict.
- Human relationships.

Themes SHOULD influence narrative decisions, character motivations, and worldbuilding.

Designers SHOULD avoid introducing themes that have no meaningful relationship to the actual story or player experience.

### 4.3 Narrative Tone

Tone defines the emotional and stylistic character of the narrative.

Examples include:

- Serious.
- Tragic.
- Hopeful.
- Humorous.
- Mysterious.
- Satirical.
- Melancholic.
- Suspenseful.
- Absurdist.

Tone SHOULD remain coherent across dialogue, character behavior, visual presentation, music, and narrative events.

Tone MAY change intentionally when supported by the story and player experience.

### 4.4 Narrative Identity

The narrative SHOULD contribute to what makes the game recognizable.

Designers SHOULD consider:

- What makes the setting distinctive.
- What conflicts define the story.
- What perspectives the narrative explores.
- What distinguishes its characters.
- What makes its themes relevant to the experience.

Narrative identity SHOULD emerge from coherent creative decisions rather than superficial references to existing works.

## 5. Story Structure

### 5.1 Story Premise

A story premise SHOULD communicate the fundamental situation that drives the narrative.

It SHOULD identify:

- The protagonist or central perspective.
- The setting.
- The central conflict or dramatic situation.
- The primary narrative objective.
- The principal source of tension.

The premise SHOULD be understandable without requiring extensive background information.

### 5.2 Narrative Structure

Narrative structure SHOULD organize events into a coherent experience.

Possible structures include:

- Linear.
- Branching.
- Episodic.
- Nonlinear.
- Open-ended.
- Environmental.
- Emergent.
- Hybrid.

The chosen structure SHOULD reflect the intended experience and available production resources.

No narrative structure is inherently superior to another.

### 5.3 Narrative Progression

Narrative progression SHOULD establish a meaningful relationship between events.

Designers SHOULD consider:

- Introduction of the central situation.
- Development of conflict.
- Character motivations.
- Important discoveries.
- Escalation or transformation.
- Consequences.
- Resolution or deliberate ambiguity.

Not every narrative requires a traditional beginning, middle, and ending, but the experience SHOULD have an intentional structure.

### 5.4 Pacing

Narrative pacing SHOULD balance information delivery, dramatic development, and gameplay.

Designers SHOULD consider:

- Frequency of narrative events.
- Length of dialogue sequences.
- Player control during important moments.
- Opportunities for reflection.
- Transitions between gameplay and exposition.
- Repetition of narrative information.
- Changes in emotional intensity.

Narrative sequences SHOULD NOT unnecessarily interrupt gameplay or remove player control without a clear design reason.

### 5.5 Narrative Information

Information SHOULD be revealed at an appropriate point in the experience.

Designers SHOULD distinguish between:

- Information the player must understand.
- Information that improves context.
- Optional background information.
- Deliberately hidden information.
- Information intended to remain ambiguous.

Important narrative information SHOULD be accessible through an appropriate combination of presentation and discovery.

## 6. Characters and Character Development

### 6.1 Character Purpose

Every significant character SHOULD have a narrative or gameplay purpose.

Characters MAY serve as:

- Protagonists.
- Antagonists.
- Allies.
- Mentors.
- Rivals.
- Supporting characters.
- Quest providers.
- Sources of information.
- Representatives of factions or groups.
- Environmental or narrative figures.

Characters SHOULD contribute to the world, conflict, themes, or player experience.

### 6.2 Character Definition

Significant characters SHOULD have clearly defined:

- Identity.
- Background.
- Personality.
- Motivations.
- Goals.
- Fears or limitations.
- Beliefs and values.
- Relationships.
- Conflicts.
- Relevant knowledge.
- Narrative role.

Character details SHOULD remain consistent with established world rules.

### 6.3 Character Motivation

Character actions SHOULD be supported by understandable motivations.

Motivations MAY be:

- Personal.
- Social.
- Political.
- Economic.
- Emotional.
- Ideological.
- Survival-related.
- Conflicting or contradictory.

Characters SHOULD NOT act solely because the plot requires an arbitrary action when a coherent motivation can be established.

### 6.4 Character Development

Character development SHOULD result from meaningful experiences, decisions, relationships, and consequences.

Development MAY involve:

- Changes in beliefs.
- Shifts in priorities.
- Personal growth.
- Moral compromise.
- Loss.
- New understanding.
- Increased responsibility.
- Deteriorating relationships.

Not every character requires a dramatic transformation. Static characters MAY be effective when their role is intentionally designed.

### 6.5 Character Relationships

Relationships SHOULD have understandable foundations and relevant dynamics.

Designers SHOULD consider:

- Shared history.
- Conflicting objectives.
- Trust.
- Loyalty.
- Dependence.
- Rivalry.
- Power differences.
- Emotional connections.

Relationships SHOULD influence character behavior and narrative situations when relevant.

## 7. Dialogue and Conversations

### 7.1 Dialogue Purpose

Dialogue SHOULD serve one or more clear purposes:

- Advance the story.
- Reveal character.
- Communicate relevant information.
- Establish relationships.
- Create emotional impact.
- Provide context.
- Support gameplay.
- Reinforce world identity.
- Offer meaningful player choices.

Dialogue SHOULD avoid unnecessary exposition and repetition.

### 7.2 Character Voice

Significant characters SHOULD have distinguishable voices.

Voice MAY be established through:

- Vocabulary.
- Sentence structure.
- Formality.
- Humor.
- Emotional expression.
- Cultural context.
- Speech patterns.
- Communication habits.

Dialogue SHOULD reflect the character's personality, background, circumstances, and knowledge.

### 7.3 Dialogue Clarity

Dialogue SHOULD be understandable within its intended context.

Designers SHOULD:

- Avoid unnecessary complexity.
- Preserve relevant context.
- Introduce unfamiliar terminology carefully.
- Avoid excessive exposition.
- Ensure important information is not easily missed.
- Keep conversations appropriate to their purpose.

### 7.4 Dialogue Choices

When dialogue choices are provided, their intended differences SHOULD be understandable.

Choices MAY represent:

- Different attitudes.
- Different information requests.
- Different intentions.
- Relationship changes.
- Narrative decisions.
- Gameplay consequences.

When choices have significant consequences, the player SHOULD receive enough contextual information to make an informed decision without necessarily knowing every future outcome.

### 7.5 Dialogue Length and Pacing

Dialogue SHOULD respect the player's time and attention.

Designers SHOULD evaluate:

- Number of lines.
- Reading time.
- Frequency of interruptions.
- Repeated conversations.
- Availability of optional dialogue.
- Whether important information can be revisited.

Where appropriate, players SHOULD be able to advance, skip, or review dialogue according to the intended experience.

## 8. Worldbuilding and Lore

### 8.1 World Identity

Worldbuilding SHOULD establish a coherent setting that supports the narrative and gameplay.

The world MAY define:

- Geography.
- History.
- Cultures.
- Social structures.
- Political systems.
- Economies.
- Belief systems.
- Technology.
- Magic or supernatural rules.
- Environmental conditions.
- Factions.
- Customs and traditions.

Worldbuilding SHOULD prioritize details relevant to the actual game experience.

### 8.2 Internal Consistency

World rules MUST remain consistent unless a deliberate narrative event establishes a change.

Designers SHOULD document important rules and constraints.

For example:

- What is possible within the setting?
- What is impossible?
- What resources are available?
- How do institutions operate?
- What technologies or abilities exist?
- What limitations govern them?

Worldbuilding SHOULD NOT introduce contradictory explanations without narrative justification.

### 8.3 Lore Management

Lore is background information that provides context to the setting, characters, and events.

Lore MAY be communicated through:

- Dialogue.
- Documents.
- Objects.
- Environmental details.
- Optional discoveries.
- Historical records.
- Visual references.
- Gameplay events.

Lore SHOULD enrich the experience without becoming an unnecessary prerequisite for understanding essential gameplay.

### 8.4 Information Hierarchy

World information SHOULD be organized according to its relevance.

A useful distinction is:

- Essential: required to understand the current situation.
- Supporting: improves understanding of characters or events.
- Optional: rewards curiosity and exploration.
- Deep background: expands the fictional setting without being required for the main experience.

Designers SHOULD avoid overwhelming players with background information before it becomes relevant.

### 8.5 Cultural and Historical Inspiration

When fictional worlds draw inspiration from real cultures, historical events, or communities, designers SHOULD approach the source material responsibly.

Research SHOULD distinguish between:

- Verified historical information.
- Cultural traditions.
- Historical interpretations.
- Fictional adaptations.
- Invented elements.

Creative adaptations SHOULD be intentional and internally coherent.

Designers SHOULD avoid reducing complex cultures or historical communities to superficial stereotypes.

## 9. Environmental Storytelling

### 9.1 Narrative Through Environment

The environment MAY communicate narrative information through its appearance, composition, and interactive elements.

Examples include:

- Abandoned structures.
- Damaged objects.
- Personal belongings.
- Architectural differences.
- Environmental changes.
- Evidence of previous events.
- Character locations.
- Objects arranged to imply relationships or activity.

Environmental storytelling SHOULD allow players to discover information through observation and interaction.

### 9.2 Narrative Consistency

Environmental details SHOULD be consistent with established world history and current events.

The placement and condition of objects SHOULD make sense within their context unless a deliberate narrative purpose justifies otherwise.

### 9.3 Player Interpretation

Environmental storytelling MAY allow multiple interpretations.

Designers SHOULD distinguish between:

- Deliberate ambiguity.
- Unclear communication.
- Missing narrative context.

Ambiguity SHOULD encourage interpretation rather than result from accidental inconsistency.

Spatial composition and environmental navigation SHOULD follow `level_design_guidelines.md`.

## 10. Player Choices and Narrative Consequences

### 10.1 Meaningful Choices

Narrative choices SHOULD represent meaningful differences in player intention, relationships, or outcomes.

A choice MAY affect:

- Character relationships.
- Access to information.
- Available opportunities.
- World conditions.
- Future events.
- Narrative perspective.
- Character survival.
- Ending conditions.

Not every choice requires a major branching storyline.

### 10.2 Choice Clarity

Players SHOULD understand the immediate context of a decision.

Designers SHOULD consider:

- What the player knows.
- What information is missing intentionally.
- What motivations are involved.
- What immediate consequences are possible.
- Whether the choice is reversible.

Uncertainty MAY be part of the narrative experience, but accidental ambiguity SHOULD be avoided.

### 10.3 Consequence Management

Significant choices SHOULD produce consequences consistent with the game's rules and narrative logic.

Consequences MAY be:

- Immediate.
- Delayed.
- Direct.
- Indirect.
- Visible.
- Hidden.
- Personal.
- World-changing.

If a decision is presented as important, the player SHOULD receive meaningful evidence that it mattered.

### 10.4 Branching Narrative

Branching narratives SHOULD account for their production and maintenance costs.

Designers SHOULD evaluate:

- Number of narrative branches.
- Shared content between branches.
- Branch convergence.
- Character and world-state changes.
- Required dialogue variations.
- Testing complexity.
- Continuity risks.

Branching SHOULD be used when its contribution to the player experience justifies the additional scope.

### 10.5 Narrative State Consistency

Narrative state MUST remain coherent across events and systems.

For example:

- A character's death SHOULD be reflected in relevant future interactions.
- A completed narrative decision SHOULD NOT be unintentionally repeated.
- Dialogue SHOULD account for established player progress.
- World changes SHOULD reflect relevant previous events.

Narrative state SHOULD be managed consistently with the game's technical architecture and persistence requirements.

## 11. Narrative Integration with Gameplay

### 11.1 Narrative Through Player Actions

Whenever appropriate, narrative SHOULD emerge from player actions and their consequences.

Examples include:

- Helping or abandoning another character.
- Choosing how to approach a situation.
- Interacting with significant objects.
- Exploring a location.
- Managing scarce resources.
- Experiencing the consequences of gameplay decisions.

Player participation can communicate narrative meaning more effectively than exposition alone.

### 11.2 Narrative and Mechanics

Narrative content SHOULD be consistent with the mechanics that support it.

Designers SHOULD evaluate whether:

- Mechanics reinforce narrative themes.
- Character abilities fit their characterization.
- Gameplay consequences support narrative events.
- Player actions are consistent with the protagonist's role.
- System behavior contradicts established story rules.

When a mechanic intentionally creates narrative tension, its rules and consequences SHOULD be clearly designed.

### 11.3 Narrative and Progression

Narrative progression MAY be connected to:

- Gameplay milestones.
- New locations.
- Character relationships.
- Major discoveries.
- Player achievements.
- Changes in the world.
- Access to new mechanics.

Narrative progression SHOULD remain understandable even when players complete optional activities in different orders.

### 11.4 Avoiding Forced Exposition

Narrative delivery SHOULD minimize unnecessary interruptions to gameplay.

Designers SHOULD consider:

- Environmental storytelling.
- Optional dialogue.
- Interactive objects.
- Contextual information.
- Short narrative sequences.
- Player-controlled pacing.

Cutscenes and extended dialogue MAY be appropriate when their emotional or narrative value justifies removing player control temporarily.

## 12. Narrative Pacing and Emotional Experience

### 12.1 Emotional Intent

Significant narrative sequences SHOULD have an intended emotional purpose.

Examples include:

- Tension.
- Relief.
- Curiosity.
- Sadness.
- Hope.
- Fear.
- Empathy.
- Surprise.
- Triumph.

The intended emotional experience SHOULD be supported by character behavior, context, pacing, and presentation.

### 12.2 Emotional Variation

Narrative experiences SHOULD consider variation in emotional intensity.

Continuous high-intensity sequences MAY reduce the impact of important events.

Designers SHOULD consider opportunities for:

- Reflection.
- Recovery.
- Character interaction.
- Exploration.
- Anticipation.
- Contrast.
- Emotional resolution.

### 12.3 Narrative Transitions

Transitions between major narrative events SHOULD provide sufficient context.

Transitions MAY be communicated through:

- Gameplay.
- Dialogue.
- Environmental changes.
- Time progression.
- Character behavior.
- Audio and visual presentation.

Narrative transitions SHOULD avoid unnecessary confusion about chronology, location, or character motivations.

## 13. Narrative Accessibility and Localization

### 13.1 Accessibility

Narrative information SHOULD be accessible through appropriate presentation options.

Consider:

- Subtitles.
- Adjustable text size.
- Dialogue history.
- Speaker identification.
- Readable contrast.
- Appropriate text duration.
- Audio and visual alternatives.
- Clear presentation of important information.

Essential narrative information SHOULD NOT depend exclusively on a single sensory channel when reasonable alternatives can be provided.

### 13.2 Localization

Narrative content SHOULD be designed with localization requirements in mind.

Designers SHOULD:

- Avoid unnecessary dependence on untranslatable wordplay.
- Provide context for cultural references.
- Maintain consistent terminology.
- Allow for text expansion.
- Identify gendered or context-sensitive language.
- Avoid embedding dialogue directly into images when practical.
- Keep localized text separate from gameplay logic.

Localization SHOULD preserve narrative meaning, character voice, and intended tone rather than relying exclusively on literal translation.

## 14. Narrative Documentation

Significant narrative content SHOULD be documented sufficiently to preserve consistency throughout development.

Relevant documentation MAY include:

- Narrative vision.
- Story premise.
- Themes and tone.
- Story outline.
- Narrative structure.
- Character profiles.
- Relationship maps.
- Worldbuilding references.
- Timeline and chronology.
- Faction descriptions.
- Dialogue specifications.
- Narrative event sequences.
- Choice and consequence definitions.
- Narrative state definitions.
- Lore references.
- Open narrative questions.

Documentation SHOULD distinguish between:

- Approved narrative facts.
- Proposed narrative ideas.
- Unresolved questions.
- Deprecated content.
- Optional interpretations.

The project owner SHOULD be able to identify which narrative decisions are authoritative.

## 15. Narrative Validation and Review

### 15.1 Narrative Validation

Narrative content SHOULD be evaluated against its intended purpose.

Review SHOULD consider:

- Is the story understandable?
- Are character motivations coherent?
- Are narrative events logically connected?
- Is the tone consistent?
- Does dialogue sound appropriate for each character?
- Does worldbuilding support the setting?
- Does narrative information reach the player appropriately?
- Does gameplay support the narrative?
- Are choices and consequences meaningful?
- Are established facts preserved?

### 15.2 Playtesting

Narrative playtesting SHOULD observe how players understand and experience the story.

Useful observations include:

- Whether players understand important events.
- Whether character motivations are recognizable.
- Whether players notice relevant environmental clues.
- Whether dialogue is too long or repetitive.
- Whether narrative choices are understandable.
- Whether players care about important characters or outcomes.
- Whether narrative sequences interrupt gameplay unnecessarily.
- Whether players form interpretations consistent with the intended experience.

### 15.3 Continuity Review

Narrative reviews SHOULD verify consistency across all relevant content.

Reviewers SHOULD examine:

- Timeline conflicts.
- Character knowledge.
- Relationship continuity.
- Contradictory dialogue.
- Inconsistent world rules.
- Missing consequences.
- Repeated narrative events.
- Incorrect references to previous events.

A centralized source of truth SHOULD be maintained for important narrative facts.

## 16. AI-Assisted Narrative Design

AI agents assisting with narrative design MUST:

1. Read approved game-specific narrative documentation before proposing changes.
2. Follow these reusable narrative guidelines.
3. Preserve established characters, world rules, themes, and events.
4. Distinguish approved facts from proposed ideas.
5. Maintain consistent terminology and chronology.
6. Identify assumptions and unresolved narrative questions.
7. Evaluate the consequences of significant story changes.
8. Avoid introducing contradictions with established narrative content.
9. Preserve character voice and motivation.
10. Document significant narrative changes.
11. Request human approval for changes to approved narrative direction.

AI agents MUST NOT:

- Silently rewrite established narrative facts.
- Invent historical or cultural information and present it as verified.
- Treat generated dialogue as automatically consistent with established characters.
- Claim narrative content has been playtested without actual testing.
- Make significant narrative decisions on behalf of the project owner.

AI-generated narrative content MUST be reviewed before being treated as authoritative project material.

## 17. Narrative Design Review Checklist

### Narrative Vision
- [ ] The narrative supports the game's intended experience.
- [ ] The premise is understandable.
- [ ] Themes and tone are established where applicable.
- [ ] The central conflict or dramatic situation is defined.
- [ ] Narrative scope is appropriate to production constraints.

### Story Structure
- [ ] Narrative progression is intentional.
- [ ] Events are logically connected.
- [ ] Pacing supports the intended experience.
- [ ] Important information is communicated appropriately.
- [ ] Narrative transitions are understandable.

### Characters and Dialogue
- [ ] Significant characters have clear purposes.
- [ ] Character motivations are coherent.
- [ ] Character relationships are understood.
- [ ] Character voices are distinguishable.
- [ ] Dialogue serves a meaningful purpose.
- [ ] Dialogue choices are understandable where applicable.

### Worldbuilding
- [ ] World rules are consistent.
- [ ] Important lore is documented.
- [ ] Cultural and historical references are researched where necessary.
- [ ] Environmental storytelling supports the setting.
- [ ] Narrative facts do not contradict established information.

### Gameplay Integration
- [ ] Narrative and gameplay are coherent.
- [ ] Player actions support the intended narrative experience.
- [ ] Choices have appropriate consequences.
- [ ] Narrative state is consistent.
- [ ] Exposition does not unnecessarily interrupt gameplay.

### Validation and Production
- [ ] Narrative content has been reviewed.
- [ ] Important continuity risks are evaluated.
- [ ] Playtesting criteria are defined where appropriate.
- [ ] Accessibility requirements are considered.
- [ ] Localization requirements are considered.
- [ ] Approved narrative decisions are documented.

## 19. Maintenance

These guidelines SHOULD be reviewed periodically and updated when:

- New narrative design practices are adopted.
- Existing guidelines become unclear or contradictory.
- Repeated narrative design problems reveal missing guidance.
- Changes to the studio's development philosophy affect narrative design.
- New narrative production or localization requirements emerge.

Updates MUST preserve the document's reusable nature and avoid introducing requirements that apply exclusively to a single game.

The project owner is responsible for approving changes to these studio-wide guidelines.
