# Game Design Principles

## 1. Purpose

This document establishes the foundational principles that guide game design decisions across the studio.

Its purpose is to provide a consistent framework for designing engaging, coherent, accessible, and meaningful gameplay experiences.

These principles guide the creation and evaluation of:

- Game concepts and identities.
- Player experiences.
- Gameplay mechanics and systems.
- Core gameplay loops.
- Player interactions and decisions.
- Progression and rewards.
- Challenges and difficulty.
- Game content and activities.
- User experience and accessibility.

This document defines general design principles rather than prescribing specific mechanics, genres, or implementation approaches.

## 2. Scope

These principles apply throughout the game development lifecycle, including:

- Concept development.
- Pre-production.
- Prototyping.
- Production.
- Testing and refinement.
- Release preparation.
- Post-release development.

They apply to all game projects developed by the studio, regardless of genre, platform, visual style, or technical scope.

Game-specific design decisions MUST remain consistent with the approved vision and objectives of their respective projects.

## 3. Foundational Design Principles

### 3.1 Player Experience First

The player experience is the primary consideration in game design.

Every significant design decision SHOULD contribute to the intended experience.

Designers SHOULD evaluate mechanics, systems, content, and interactions based on how they affect the player rather than solely on their technical complexity or implementation effort.

Consider:

- What should the player feel?
- What should the player understand?
- What decisions should the player make?
- What challenges should the player overcome?
- What makes the experience memorable?
- What motivates the player to continue?

A technically impressive feature does not automatically improve a game.

### 3.2 Clear Game Identity

Every game SHOULD have a recognizable identity.

Its mechanics, interactions, presentation, progression, and content SHOULD reinforce a coherent experience.

Design decisions SHOULD support the game's established:

- Premise and setting.
- Genre and gameplay direction.
- Target audience.
- Player role and fantasy.
- Core experience.
- Distinctive characteristics.

Avoid introducing features simply because they are popular in other games.

A feature MUST have a clear purpose within the current game's identity.

### 3.3 Meaningful Player Agency

Players SHOULD have opportunities to make decisions that influence their experience.

Meaningful agency MAY involve:

- Choosing between alternative actions.
- Developing different strategies.
- Managing resources.
- Exploring different approaches.
- Accepting risks.
- Interacting with the environment.
- Influencing outcomes.
- Expressing personal preferences through gameplay.

Choices SHOULD have understandable consequences.

Not every action needs to produce a major outcome, but important decisions SHOULD provide meaningful differences.

### 3.4 Meaningful Gameplay

Gameplay activities SHOULD provide a reason for player engagement.

A mechanic SHOULD contribute at least one meaningful element, such as:

- Challenge.
- Decision-making.
- Exploration.
- Discovery.
- Expression.
- Strategy.
- Progression.
- Interaction.
- Narrative experience.
- Player satisfaction.

Avoid mechanics that add complexity without improving the intended experience.

### 3.5 Coherent Systems

Game systems SHOULD operate according to consistent rules and communicate those rules clearly.

Related mechanics SHOULD reinforce one another rather than produce contradictory or unintuitive behavior.

Designers SHOULD consider:

- System dependencies.
- Shared resources.
- Conflicting mechanics.
- Feedback consistency.
- Player expectations.
- Emergent interactions.

A system MUST be evaluated within the context of the complete gameplay experience, not exclusively in isolation.

### 3.6 Simplicity with Depth

Prefer understandable mechanics that create interesting interactions.

A simple mechanic MAY produce substantial depth through:

- Strategic choices.
- Multiple applications.
- Environmental interactions.
- Resource constraints.
- Skill expression.
- Combinations with other mechanics.

Complexity SHOULD emerge from meaningful interactions rather than unnecessary rules, exceptions, or procedural steps.

### 3.7 Consistency and Predictability

Game rules SHOULD behave consistently.

Players SHOULD be able to develop an understanding of how the game works through observation, feedback, and experience.

Exceptions MAY exist when they serve a deliberate design purpose and are communicated appropriately.

Avoid inconsistent outcomes that undermine player trust or make important mechanics difficult to understand.

## 4. Player Motivation and Engagement

### 4.1 Understand Player Motivation

Game design SHOULD consider why players engage with the experience and what motivates them to continue.

Potential motivations include:

- Mastery and skill improvement.
- Exploration and discovery.
- Achievement and accomplishment.
- Creativity and self-expression.
- Competition.
- Cooperation.
- Narrative engagement.
- Collection and completion.
- Problem-solving.
- Social interaction.

A game MAY support multiple motivations, but they SHOULD remain compatible with its intended identity.

### 4.2 Engagement Through Gameplay

Engagement SHOULD primarily come from meaningful gameplay experiences rather than artificial retention mechanisms.

Designers SHOULD avoid relying excessively on:

- Unnecessary waiting.
- Repetitive tasks without meaningful variation.
- Artificial progression restrictions.
- Excessive grinding.
- Arbitrary reward delays.
- Punitive systems designed primarily to force continued play.

Repetition MAY be appropriate when it supports mastery, strategy, or the intended gameplay experience.

### 4.3 Challenge and Mastery

Games SHOULD provide opportunities for players to learn, improve, and overcome challenges.

Challenge SHOULD be appropriate to the intended audience and the capabilities available to the player.

Designers SHOULD consider:

- Learning opportunities.
- Skill development.
- Difficulty progression.
- Player feedback.
- Opportunities to recover.
- Recognition of player improvement.

Failure SHOULD provide a reasonable opportunity to understand what happened and adapt when appropriate.

### 4.4 Emotional Experience

Game design SHOULD consider the emotions produced by gameplay.

These MAY include:

- Excitement.
- Curiosity.
- Tension.
- Satisfaction.
- Wonder.
- Fear.
- Frustration.
- Relief.
- Accomplishment.
- Empathy.

Emotional experiences SHOULD be intentional and consistent with the game's identity.

## 5. Core Gameplay and Mechanics

### 5.1 Core Gameplay Priority

The core gameplay experience SHOULD receive priority over secondary features.

The primary gameplay loop MUST be sufficiently understood and validated before extensive investment in supporting systems.

Supporting features SHOULD enhance, extend, or complement the core experience.

### 5.2 Mechanics Must Have a Purpose

Every significant mechanic SHOULD have a clearly defined gameplay purpose.

Before introducing a mechanic, evaluate:

- What problem or opportunity does it address?
- What does it allow the player to do?
- What decisions does it create?
- How does it interact with existing mechanics?
- What does it contribute to the intended experience?
- Is its complexity justified?

Avoid mechanics that exist only because they are technically interesting or easy to implement.

### 5.3 Player Feedback

Players SHOULD receive appropriate feedback for important actions and outcomes.

Feedback MAY use:

- Visual indicators.
- Animation.
- Sound.
- Text.
- Interface elements.
- Environmental changes.
- Character reactions.
- Haptic feedback, where supported.

Feedback SHOULD be timely, understandable, and consistent with the significance of the event.

### 5.4 Responsiveness

Player input SHOULD produce predictable and appropriately responsive results.

Controls and interactions SHOULD be designed around the intended gameplay requirements.

Evaluate:

- Input clarity.
- Response time.
- Control consistency.
- Interaction affordances.
- Error prevention.
- Accessibility.
- Platform-specific expectations.

## 6. Progression and Rewards

### 6.1 Purposeful Progression

Progression SHOULD provide meaningful changes to the player's experience.

It MAY introduce:

- New abilities.
- New mechanics.
- New environments.
- Additional challenges.
- Improved capabilities.
- New strategic options.
- Narrative developments.
- Access to new activities.

Progression SHOULD reinforce the core gameplay rather than merely increase numerical values.

### 6.2 Meaningful Rewards

Rewards SHOULD reflect the player's actions, effort, achievements, or decisions.

Rewards MAY be:

- Mechanical.
- Numerical.
- Narrative.
- Cosmetic.
- Exploratory.
- Social.
- Experiential.

The reward structure SHOULD support the intended player motivation.

### 6.3 Respect Player Time

Player time SHOULD be treated as a valuable resource.

Activities SHOULD provide appropriate value relative to their duration and effort.

Designers SHOULD evaluate:

- Time required to complete objectives.
- Time between meaningful decisions.
- Time required for progression.
- Repetitive activities.
- Waiting periods.
- Opportunities to pause or resume.

Long activities MAY be appropriate when their duration contributes meaningfully to the intended experience.

## 7. Difficulty, Fairness, and Balance

### 7.1 Fair Challenge

Challenges SHOULD be demanding without relying on arbitrary or unintelligible rules.

Players SHOULD have reasonable opportunities to understand threats, evaluate options, and respond.

Difficulty SHOULD reflect the intended game experience rather than arbitrary numerical escalation.

### 7.2 Consistent Rules

The game SHOULD communicate and apply its mechanics consistently.

Players SHOULD not be punished for failing to understand rules that were never reasonably communicated.

Uncertainty MAY be intentional, but it SHOULD create interesting decisions rather than unexplained outcomes.

### 7.3 Meaningful Trade-offs

When players choose between abilities, equipment, resources, or strategies, alternatives SHOULD offer meaningful trade-offs where appropriate.

Avoid situations where one option consistently invalidates all others without a deliberate design reason.

### 7.4 Balance Validation

Balance decisions SHOULD be evaluated through appropriate calculations, simulations, and gameplay testing.

Numerical equality does not necessarily produce an enjoyable or fair experience.

Refer to `balancing_guidelines.md` for detailed balancing practices.

## 8. Exploration and Discovery

When exploration and discovery are part of the game experience, they SHOULD provide meaningful opportunities for player curiosity.

Designers SHOULD consider:

- Discoverable locations.
- Environmental storytelling.
- Hidden content.
- Interesting interactions.
- Navigation and orientation.
- Meaningful secrets.
- Information discovery.
- Rewards for exploration.

Discovery SHOULD feel connected to the world and gameplay.

Avoid excessive map clutter, arbitrary secrets, or exploration requirements that provide little value to the player.

## 9. Narrative and World Design

When narrative is part of the experience, it SHOULD complement gameplay.

Narrative elements MAY be delivered through:

- Dialogue.
- Environmental storytelling.
- Events.
- Characters.
- Gameplay consequences.
- Visual design.
- Exploration.
- Player choices.

The relationship between narrative and mechanics SHOULD be coherent.

### 9.1 World Consistency

The game's world SHOULD follow recognizable internal rules.

Characters, environments, events, and mechanics SHOULD be consistent with the established setting and tone.

Exceptions SHOULD have a clear narrative or gameplay justification.

### 9.2 Narrative Integration

Narrative SHOULD be integrated into the gameplay experience where appropriate.

Avoid unnecessary interruptions that prevent players from engaging with the primary activity.

The amount of narrative direction SHOULD reflect the intended experience and level of player agency.

## 10. Usability and Accessibility

### 10.1 Usability

Gameplay interactions and interface elements SHOULD be understandable and usable.

Designers SHOULD consider:

- Clear controls.
- Understandable objectives.
- Consistent interface behavior.
- Readable information.
- Appropriate feedback.
- Clear navigation.
- Error prevention.
- Appropriate onboarding.

### 10.2 Accessibility

Accessibility SHOULD be considered from the beginning of design rather than treated exclusively as a late production adjustment.

Where appropriate, consider:

- Text readability.
- Color contrast.
- Color-independent information.
- Subtitles and captions.
- Remappable controls.
- Input alternatives.
- Adjustable difficulty.
- Audio and visual accessibility options.
- Reduced-motion options.
- Clear communication of important gameplay information.

Accessibility decisions SHOULD reflect the game's target platforms, audience, and technical constraints.

## 11. Game Content and World Cohesion

### 11.1 Purposeful Content

Every significant piece of content SHOULD contribute to the experience.

Content MAY provide:

- Gameplay variety.
- Challenge.
- Exploration.
- Narrative context.
- Progression.
- Player expression.
- World-building.

Avoid adding content solely to increase the apparent size of the game.

### 11.2 Consistent Presentation

Visuals, audio, interface, writing, and gameplay SHOULD reinforce a coherent identity.

Presentation decisions SHOULD support readability, usability, atmosphere, and player understanding.

### 11.3 Content Reuse

Reusable content and systems MAY improve production efficiency.

However, reuse SHOULD preserve meaningful variety and quality.

Avoid excessive repetition that makes the world or gameplay feel artificial.

## 12. Scope and Design Prioritization

### 12.1 Design Within Project Constraints

Game design decisions MUST consider the project's available resources and constraints.

These MAY include:

- Development time.
- Team size.
- Technical capabilities.
- Budget.
- Target platform.
- Asset availability.
- Production complexity.
- Maintenance requirements.

A design is not viable merely because it is theoretically desirable.

### 12.2 Prioritize Experience

When deciding between features, prioritize those that contribute most directly to the intended player experience.

Consider:

- Contribution to the core gameplay.
- Player impact.
- Design uniqueness.
- Development effort.
- Technical risk.
- Dependencies.
- Long-term maintenance cost.

Secondary features SHOULD NOT delay validation of essential gameplay.

### 12.3 Avoid Feature Creep

New features SHOULD be evaluated against the approved game vision and current project priorities.

A feature SHOULD NOT be introduced simply because it is feasible or interesting.

When a proposed feature expands scope, evaluate whether its expected value justifies the additional development and maintenance effort.

### 12.4 Prototype Before Committing

Uncertain or high-risk design ideas SHOULD be tested through prototypes before major implementation investment.

Prototypes SHOULD answer specific design questions.

Avoid building complete systems when a smaller experiment can validate the underlying assumption.

## 13. Design Validation and Iteration

Game design SHOULD be treated as an iterative process.

Design assumptions MUST be validated at an appropriate level before being treated as established facts.

### 13.1 Design Hypotheses

Significant design decisions SHOULD identify:

- The problem or opportunity.
- The proposed solution.
- The expected player impact.
- The assumptions involved.
- The validation method.
- The expected outcome.

### 13.2 Playtesting

Playtesting SHOULD evaluate actual player behavior and experience.

Observe:

- Player understanding.
- Player decisions.
- Engagement.
- Confusion.
- Difficulty.
- Unexpected strategies.
- Repetition.
- Feedback effectiveness.

### 13.3 Iterative Improvement

Designers SHOULD use test results to refine mechanics, interactions, and content.

Changes SHOULD address identified problems rather than introduce unrelated complexity.

### 13.4 Preserve Validated Decisions

Validated design decisions SHOULD remain stable unless new evidence or project requirements justify revisiting them.

Changes to approved design direction SHOULD be documented and reviewed.

## 14. Design Decision-Making

When evaluating a significant design decision, consider the following questions:

1. Does it support the approved game vision?
2. Does it improve the intended player experience?
3. Does it provide meaningful gameplay value?
4. Does it introduce meaningful decisions or interactions?
5. Does it work coherently with existing systems?
6. Is its complexity justified?
7. Is it feasible within project constraints?
8. Can its assumptions be tested?
9. Does it introduce unnecessary risks or dependencies?
10. Is there a simpler approach that achieves the same result?

Not every decision requires a formal document. The level of analysis and documentation SHOULD be proportional to its impact.

## 15. AI-Assisted Game Design Requirements

AI agents assisting with game design MUST follow these requirements:

1. Read the approved game vision and relevant project-specific design documentation.
2. Apply these principles when proposing mechanics, systems, and content.
3. Preserve the established game identity and intended player experience.
4. Avoid introducing features without a clear design purpose.
5. Distinguish design assumptions from validated findings.
6. Identify dependencies and potential interactions with existing systems.
7. Consider player agency, usability, accessibility, and scope.
8. Recommend prototypes when design uncertainty is significant.
9. Avoid expanding scope without justification.
10. Maintain consistency with approved design decisions.
11. Clearly identify proposed changes to existing design direction.
12. Seek project owner approval before making significant changes to approved game vision or design direction.

AI-generated design proposals are not approved decisions until reviewed by the project owner.

## 16. Game Design Review Checklist

Use this checklist when evaluating significant design proposals or gameplay systems.

- [ ] The design supports the approved game vision.
- [ ] Its intended player experience is clear.
- [ ] Its gameplay purpose is defined.
- [ ] It provides meaningful player interaction or value.
- [ ] Player decisions and consequences are understandable.
- [ ] It works coherently with existing systems.
- [ ] Its complexity is justified.
- [ ] It provides appropriate feedback.
- [ ] Its difficulty and rewards are appropriate.
- [ ] Usability and accessibility have been considered.
- [ ] Its production scope is realistic.
- [ ] Its assumptions and risks are identified.
- [ ] Its design can be validated through appropriate testing.
- [ ] Relevant decisions are documented.

Not every item applies to every design decision. Non-applicable items SHOULD be identified rather than treated as mandatory work.

## 18. Maintenance

These principles SHOULD be reviewed when:

- The studio's design philosophy changes.
- New design practices are adopted.
- Repeated design problems reveal gaps in the principles.
- New tools or techniques materially improve design workflows.

Changes MUST preserve the document's purpose as a reusable foundation for game design rather than turning it into a specification for a particular game.

The project owner is responsible for approving changes to studio-wide game design principles.
